# Build on hand_v2, gate hard, upload once

As of 14:16 UTC there are about 2 h 45 min left, roughly **260 live games** at ≈95 games per hour per seat. The strongest model the team can still field is not a new network trained by self-play. It is **hand_v2's exact tactical features with a small learned residual on top**, trained on search-improved targets and shaped so that its policy output works as a move-ordering prior for the referee's fixed 100-simulation PUCT search. That search makes the prior the dominant lever. A flat prior fell to **27%** in the team's sweep, and at 100 simulations a 3-logit prior lead needs about a 0.4 value advantage to be overturned. A sharper value hurt instead (v_scale 2 scored **32% overall and 10% as Cyan**), so values should be calibrated win probabilities, which hand_v2's Texel-fitted weights already largely are. Speed buys no extra search, so the only architectural rule is to stay at or below **~3 ms per batch-8 call on one core**. hand_v2 measures 0.55–1.0 ms, which leaves room for a res4x32 residual trunk. The most urgent decision is about evaluation, not training. distill_v2 and distill_v2n already score **85–87% against stopgap_v1, the same as the live hand_v2**, so stopgap can no longer separate them, and only a direct, colour-paired gate against hand_v2 can. Bundle the gains into **one candidate that clears ≥41/60 against hand_v2**. Upload it by 16:00–16:20 if the edge is about +10 pp, or by 16:30 if it is about +20 pp, and upload nothing but a rollback after 16:45. Continuing pure AlphaZero is the worst use of the remaining hours: the best az model reached only 28% against stopgap.

A few things should be dropped outright, and each has direct evidence against it. **Pure AlphaZero continuation**: the az policy loss stalled at 2.98 → 2.85, close to ln 20 ≈ 3.0, i.e. a nearly uniform prior, and the best az model scored 28% against stopgap ([az log](../agents/az/log.txt), [az RESULTS.md](../agents/az/RESULTS.md)). **Global value or prior rescaling on its own**: it was flat at 48–50% in the sweep. **Constant side-to-move value offsets**, **networks above ~3 ms per batch-8 call**, and **uploads that miss the gate or the clock cutoffs** are also out. ## Prior rank, not value sharpness, picks moves at 100 simulations

The referee's search has quirks that a model can either exploit or trip over. It runs PUCT with c_puct 1.5, 100 simulations, batch 8 and **virtual loss 1.0**, freshly for every action, so the second action of a turn gets a new tree. An unvisited edge scores **Q = 0 in absolute terms** (FPU 0), whatever its parent's value. Ties go to the first legal action in move-generation order, because the comparison is a strict `>`. **Kernel captures are terminal (+1)**: they need no evaluator call and no virtual loss, and they are backed up immediately. The root's own value is discarded, so at the root only the priors matter. Dirichlet noise is mixed in after softmax normalisation and only during the first 8 actions, so no scaling trick can game it. Two details matter most for learned values. First, the backup sign flips only at the end of a turn, so the value of an actions_left = 1 position is added **unchanged** to the same team's first-action edge. Second, the referee's own docstring says first actions are "systematically under-searched", and the `adaptive_simulations` fix for that is switched off ([mcts.py](../data/colony-one-env/colony_one/mcts.py)).

The team's sweep scaled stopgap's outputs and played each variant 60 games against the original stopgap_v1 ([sweep_results.jsonl](../sweep_results.jsonl)):

| Variant | Win rate | 95% CI | Cyan | Magenta |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| v_scale 0.5 | 48% | 36–61 | 43% | 53% |
| v_scale 2 | **32%** | 21–44 | **10%** | 53% |
| p_scale 0 (flat prior) | **27%** | 17–39 | 23% | 30% |
| p_scale 2 | 50% | 38–62 | 53% | 47% |
| p_scale 4 | 50% | 38–62 | 37% | 63% |

Both knobs are **flat-topped around 1**. Halving the value or sharpening the prior 2–4× changes nothing that 60 games can resolve (±12 pp). Removing the prior, or doubling the value, costs 18–23 pp. That asymmetry shows where the strength sits: in *which* moves the prior ranks first, not in the softmax temperature. Scaling every value by k gives roughly the same argmax as dividing c_puct by k (virtual loss and the ±1 terminals do not scale), so v_scale 2 behaves like a value-greedy c_puct of 0.75. That search trusts single-ply leaf values that are noisier than the capture prior. A NumPy replica of the referee's root selection, virtual-loss and batching loop puts numbers on this ([root_bandit.py](file:///C:/Users/melis/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/C--Users-melis-Downloads-cloudflight-coding-contest/2537b5b1-8fcc-4ac8-8fd3-b627f7b7b210/scratchpad/root_bandit.py)). In the table below, each cell is the share of 200 searches in which a truly better move with logit 0 overturned a favourite with logit lead L, given a value gap g:

| Prior lead L (P of favourite) | g = 0.05 | g = 0.10 | g = 0.20 | g = 0.40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (0.05) | 0.56 | 0.81 | 0.98 | 1.00 |
| 1 (0.13) | 0.13 | 0.34 | 0.82 | 1.00 |
| 2 (0.28) | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.18 | 0.99 |
| 3 (0.51) | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.73 |
| 4 (0.74) | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.14 |

A lead of 3–4 logits is effectively final, so near-one-hot priors belong only on forced moves. A second replica test found that **FPU 0 turns the value's sign into an exploration switch** ([root_bandit_b2.py](file:///C:/Users/melis/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/C--Users-melis-Downloads-cloudflight-coding-contest/2537b5b1-8fcc-4ac8-8fd3-b627f7b7b210/scratchpad/root_bandit_b2.py)). When the mover's visited moves score at or below about 0, every unvisited move looks better and the search goes wide. When they score **≥ +0.3**, a winning move that sits last in tie order and outside the top ~5 priors is **never visited**. Giving that move a **+0.3 logit tie-break** made it reliably found. Virtual loss produces the same effect: the first batch of 8 becomes a 1-ply scan of the top 5–8 prior-ranked moves (8 distinct moves when the top prior is 0.3, 5 when it is 0.9). The rule that follows is that **every must-consider move (kernel escape, guard step, recapture) must rank in the top ~5, and quiet moves must never tie exactly.** The v_scale 2 collapse to 10% as Cyan matches this mechanism. Cyan wins only **38% of 4,187 games** globally ([tournament-dashboard.json](../data/matches/tournament-dashboard.json)), so its calibrated values sit near −0.24 or below. Saturating them sends Cyan's own nodes maximally wide and shallow, while Magenta's nodes in the same tree narrow.

The right value target is a **calibrated win probability, 2p − 1**, because tanh(x) = 2σ(2x) − 1 and a logistic fit therefore gives the tanh scale directly. hand_v2 already does most of this. Its value weights were Texel-fitted on 21.7k positions from 600 self-play games (validation accuracy 0.70), and its policy parameters were fitted to root visit counts ([hand RESULTS.md](../agents/hand/RESULTS.md)). Unlike stopgap, it also reads plane 13 (actions left) and plane 15 (Magenta to move). Two checks remain. The first is a histogram of |v| on non-decisive positions: values above 0.95 look almost like a kernel kill and blind the side that is ahead. Decisive terms (kernel attackable now, kernel on the next ring to lock) should stay saturated. The second concerns colour: express the asymmetry as a **colour-antisymmetric term on plane 15**, which looks the same from every node's own perspective at every depth. Never use a constant side-to-move offset. That adds +c to same-team leaves at actions_left = 1 and −c to opponent leaves, so it biases lightly searched moves through an odd/even depth effect ([mcts.py](../data/colony-one-env/colony_one/mcts.py), backup at L431–438 and L546–548). For any learned value, verify that the correlation between v(parent at actions_left = 2) and v(child at actions_left = 1) is strongly positive. Training that assumes turns alternate after every action flips this sign, which is catastrophic.

Because unvisited moves are scored only by P, and the first batch spreads visits by prior rank, the logits act as a **move-ordering heuristic**, and the same prior drives the opponent's nodes, where it doubles as threat detection. This matches the literature: among MCTS–minimax hybrids that use evaluation functions, computing node priors with minimax was the strongest variant, and in Breakthrough it beat standalone enhanced minimax ([Baier & Winands, JAIR 2018](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/11208)). The replica and the stopgap prior scales ([stopgap.py](../stopgap.py)) support this layout:

| Move class | Logit above quiet baseline |
|---|---|
| Kernel capture | ≥ +5 (terminal anyway; it only needs to be visited first) |
| Capture of an undefended piece | +value/20 (Daemon 2.25, Ice 2.5) |
| Capture into a defended square | +(value − attacker)/20, floor 0 |
| Kernel move out of attack or off the next ring to lock | +2.5 |
| Guard step next to an attacked own kernel | +1.5 |
| Move onto an enemy-attacked, undefended square | −piece value/50 (≈ −1) |
| Quiet positional moves | 0–1 tie-breaks, never exact ties |

The search sees roughly 4–5 actions down its principal line and 1–2 on side lines. That is an inference from visit splitting and has not been measured. The value should therefore carry quiescence-like knowledge: the best capture available scaled by actions left, pieces en prise against the opponent's coming two actions, and kernel attackability. hand_v2's two-action kernel-strike features already cover the most important part of this.

## Search-improved targets on top of hand_v2 beat self-play from scratch

The az pipeline failed for a clear reason. Its policy loss settled near ln(#legal moves), and its value loss of 0.66 was barely below the ~0.75 a colour-only predictor would reach. The network learned little beyond "which colour am I", and inside PUCT-100 a flat prior with a weak value loses to exact hand features ([az log](../agents/az/log.txt)). Expert Iteration is the method that fits a 1–3 hour window. A tree-search expert plans, and a network generalises those plans. Imitating the search's **visit distribution rather than the chosen move gave 50 ± 13 Elo** at a near-identical top-1 error (47.0% vs 47.7%). Extra DAgger-style batches sampled with the apprentice added **120 Elo**. Online ExIt "substantially outperforms the batch mode", keeping all data was marginally better than a rolling buffer, and the final apprentice roughly matched its MCTS teacher, 87 wins out of 162 games ([Anthony et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08439)).

The team's distill pipeline is already ExIt-shaped. Its teacher is an alpha-beta negamax over the rest of the own turn plus the full opponent turn (3–4 actions), followed by a captures-only quiescence search. Policy labels are softmax(root scores / 0.5); value labels are 0.75·tanh(best/3) + 0.25·z. The student is a 32-channel, 4-block residual net **added on top of the fixed stopgap evaluator** ([distill RESULTS.md](../agents/distill/RESULTS.md)). That design rose from **67% against stopgap with only ~3k positions** (distill_v1, 80/120 pooled) to **85% for distill_v2 and 87% for distill_v2n**, and distill_v2 beat distill_v1 51/60 ([arena_results.jsonl](../agents/distill/arena_results.jsonl)). The obvious next model keeps everything and **swaps the base to hand_v2's prior and value**, with the residual heads' last layers initialised to zero, so that at step 0 the model plays exactly like hand_v2. No published study measures this zero-init "residual on a hand evaluator" trick for AlphaZero-style training. It rests on the same principle as zero-initialised residual branches and on the team's own stopgap result. The teacher can also use the hand evaluator as its static evaluation, which makes it a stronger expert in the ExIt sense.

Data volume is the cheapest multiplier. **All 8 symmetries of the board are valid for the rules**: every piece moves the same way in all directions, the firewall rings are concentric, and the bodyguard order goes by piece type ([rules.md](../data/colony-one-env/docs/rules.md)). The trainer currently applies only the anti-diagonal reflection (r,c) → (7−c, 7−r) ([train.py](../agents/distill/train.py)). That is the one transform that keeps both kernels on their start corners. The other six produce legal but off-distribution positions that still teach 8×8 tactics. Each transform must remap both squares of the policy index (from·64 + to, per [observation.md](../data/colony-one-env/docs/observation.md)) and the legal mask. At about 1 s of CPU per game, one core yields ~3,600 games per hour. 4–5 single-threaded workers therefore produce 5k–10k games, about 175k–350k positions, per round. Workers must pin OMP/MKL/torch threads to 1, because the 20 cores are shared with the live training agents.

For value targets, blend the outcome with search. Softened and bootstrapped targets "train faster and perform better" than raw z on Connect-Four and Breakthrough ([Willemsen, Baier & Kaisers 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00521-021-05928-5.pdf)), and the Lc0 discussion proposes a per-move blend that leans on Q early and on z late ([lc0 #84](https://github.com/leelachesszero/lc0/issues/84)). Drop policy targets from the 8 noisy opening actions, or prune noise-forced visits, so the network does not learn to imitate Dirichlet noise ([KataGo](https://arxiv.org/html/1902.10565v5)). KataGo's other ablations show how much compute each trick saved: playout-cap randomisation **1.37×**, forced playouts with target pruning **1.25×**, global pooling **1.60×**, an opponent-move auxiliary head **1.30×**, and **game-specific input features 1.55×** ([KataGo](https://arxiv.org/html/1902.10565v5)). These were measured over multi-day 19×19 Go runs, so only the last one transfers cleanly, and it argues for keeping hand features in the input. Gumbel AlphaZero's completed-Q target, softmax(prior logits + σ(completed Q)), stays sharp even with tiny visit counts ([mctx policies.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-deepmind/mctx/main/mctx/_src/policies.py), [qtransforms.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-deepmind/mctx/main/mctx/_src/qtransforms.py)), and it "significantly improve[s] prior performance when planning with few simulations" ([Danihelka et al., ICLR 2022](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2022/poster/6418)). It pays off only if data generation drops to 32 simulations or fewer, and no source tests a Gumbel-trained network deployed under plain PUCT.

## A 3 ms batch-8 budget fits hand_v2 plus a res4x32 trunk

The referee runs each model through ONNX Runtime on CPU with **one intra-op and one inter-op thread** and the default graph optimisations ([onnx_runner.py](../data/colony-one-env/colony_one/onnx_runner.py)). Those defaults include Conv+BN and Conv+activation fusion and the NCHWc layout, which measured **3.2× faster** than no optimisation on a res6x64 (30 → 9.5 ms) ([ORT graph optimizations](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/model-optimizations/graph-optimizations.html), [bench_arch.py](file:///C:/Users/melis/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/C--Users-melis-Downloads-cloudflight-coding-contest/2537b5b1-8fcc-4ac8-8fd3-b627f7b7b210/scratchpad/bench_arch.py)). Each action costs 14 calls (12 at batch 8, one at batch 4, one at batch 1). A maximum-length game of 26 rounds is **104 actions, 52 per seat**, so one seat makes about **700 batch-8-equivalent calls** per game. The local validator times only a Level 3 match (60 simulations against GreedyAgent) and stops at 13.5 s ([validate_submission.py](../data/colony-one-env/tools/validate_submission.py)). The Level 4 per-match limit, and whether both seats share one 15 s limit, are not published.

The search's own Python overhead is the other cost. stopgap_v1 used 1.1 s of CPU per arena game for both seats ([DEPLOYS.md](../DEPLOYS.md)), of which the models account for only ~0.2 s, which works out to roughly 25 ms of Python per action, or ~2.7 s per 104-action game. A model at 3 ms per call adds 2.1 s of its own per maximum-length game on this machine. On a server twice as slow, with one shared 15 s limit and an equally heavy opponent, the total lands near 14 s. **3 ms is therefore the hard ceiling, and 2–2.5 ms is preferred.** These are inferences from the formula; the server's CPU speed is unknown. The res-net and ray-feature rows come from the research benchmarks, [bench2.py](file:///C:/Users/melis/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/C--Users-melis-Downloads-cloudflight-coding-contest/2537b5b1-8fcc-4ac8-8fd3-b627f7b7b210/scratchpad/bench2.py) and [bench3.py](file:///C:/Users/melis/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/C--Users-melis-Downloads-cloudflight-coding-contest/2537b5b1-8fcc-4ac8-8fd3-b627f7b7b210/scratchpad/bench3.py), with about ±25% noise from contention. The rule of thumb is **~0.3 ms per million MACs per position at batch 8, plus 2–3 µs per graph node**. Since the number of simulations is fixed, a faster network buys nothing, and the budget should go into evaluation quality up to the ceiling: **hand_v2 plus a res4x32 residual lands around 2.2–2.8 ms**. Four residual blocks give 9 stacked 3×3 convolutions, enough to cover the corner-to-corner distance of 7. Depth beats width at this size: res8x32-SE costs the same as res4x48. A global-pooling bias cost only +0.02 ms, and depthwise convolutions were no faster in ORT. Lc0's distilled CPU networks are small SE towers, and 24x3-SE was reported ~300 Elo stronger than 16x2-SE ([dkappe Distilled Networks](https://github.com/dkappe/leela-chess-weights/wiki/Distilled-Networks)). For the policy head, a **1×1 conv to 64 channels, with channel = from-square and spatial position = to-square**, flattens straight into the from·64 + to index without a Transpose, which saved ~0.3 ms on res4x48. An alternative is a relative-offset head: 32 offsets (8 directions × distance 1–3 plus 8 knight jumps) mapped through one Gather on a constant index initializer. A 4096-wide fully connected head adds 525k parameters and ~3 ms ([Lc0 NN backend](https://lczero.org/dev/backend/nn/)).

The graph limits bind before file size does. The caps are **MAX_NODES 1024 and MAX_INITIALIZERS 512** ([submission_policy.py](../data/colony-one-env/colony_one/submission_policy.py)). hand_v2 already uses 469 nodes and **416 initializers**, which leaves 96 initializers for a residual trunk. Because BN folds into the convolutions, each conv costs about 2 initializers, so a res4x32 trunk with its heads (≈25–30 initializers) fits. Initializers, not file size, are the cap that binds for anything larger, which makes hand_v2fast (167 nodes, 225 initializers) the roomier base. The op allow-list has **no Constant, no Quantize/Dequantize (so INT8 is impossible), and no Pad, Expand, Tile, OneHot or Scatter**, and shape and index inputs must be initializers. Storing weights as FP16 halves the file at no latency cost, but it is irrelevant for nets under 300k parameters, given the 5 MiB cap. Use the team's export recipe: opset 17, `dynamo=False`, constant folding, `external_data=False`, a dynamic batch axis, a value output of shape (B,) ending in tanh, then `fix_constants` and the validator ([c1.py](../c1.py)). Never submit an ORT-saved optimised model: its fused nodes live in `com.microsoft` domains, which the domain allow-list rejects. Under load, the validator's 2 s check is flaky, so re-run it after a rejection ([DEPLOYS.md](../DEPLOYS.md)).

## Stopgap can no longer rank the candidates

Three models now score the same 85–87% against stopgap_v1: hand_v2, distill_v2 and distill_v2n ([hand RESULTS.md](../agents/hand/RESULTS.md), [arena_results.jsonl](../agents/distill/arena_results.jsonl)). At p ≈ 0.85 each game carries about half the Fisher information of a 50% game, since information is proportional to p(1−p). Worse, real games have a "spinning top" geometry, with many non-transitive cycles at intermediate strength ([Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)). Equal scores against a third model therefore say nothing about the head-to-head. The team has already seen saturation of this kind: hand_v1 scored 95% against seat_l3 but 52% against stopgap, and az_v1 scored 90% against seat_l3 but 10% against stopgap. **The only informative test is each candidate against hand_v2 directly.**

Only big edges can be proven in the time left. A Bernoulli SPRT of H0 = 50% against H1 at α = β = 0.10 (LLR bounds ±2.20) needs on average **21 games for a 70% edge, 87 for 60%, and 351 for 55%**. At α = β = 0.05 the averages are 32, 132 and 530 ([SPRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_probability_ratio_test); own simulation, [calc.py](file:///C:/Users/melis/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/C--Users-melis-Downloads-cloudflight-coding-contest/2537b5b1-8fcc-4ac8-8fd3-b627f7b7b210/scratchpad/calc.py)). Run the SPRT with H1 = 0.65 and cap it at 120 games. A real 55% edge will usually end as a rejection, and should: it sits below the ±45–70-point luck in the final rating. The team's fixed-n gate requires a **Wilson lower bound above 0.55: at least 41/60, 53/80 or 65/100, and at least 44/60 after 16:00**. Neither colour may fall below 50%, the score against greedy must be at least 95%, and no gauntlet member may drop by more than 10 pp ([DEPLOYS.md](../DEPLOYS.md)). hand_v2 cleared this with 68/80 (lower bound 0.756).

The referee plays greedily by visit count after 8 noisy opening actions, so two fixed models produce **few distinct games**. If 60 games contain only 15 distinct lines, the true interval is about twice as wide as the reported one. Hash each game's action list and count distinct games. Widen the arena's openings with a book of 20–40 random 2–4-ply openings, and play each opening with both colours. Score the pairs as WW / split / LL; Fishtest's pentanomial model gives "a substantial saving of testing resources" ([Fishtest Mathematics](https://official-stockfish.github.io/docs/fishtest-wiki/Fishtest-Mathematics.html)). Turn every live loss into a regression position: the position before the decisive blunder, plus the property the move must satisfy (for example, the kernel is not on the next ring to lock, or no enemy piece can capture the kernel within 2 actions). The round-8 firewall lock caused 5–8 of the old seat's 18 exported losses ([tournament-matches.json](../data/matches/tournament-matches.json)). hand_v2's 12 losses to stopgap were 11 captures and 1 firewall burn. Now that hand_v2 plays about 95 live games an hour, its own fresh replays show what the current rating stratum exploits.

## One gated upload before 16:20 beats several small ones

The live server behaves like textbook Glicko-2 with **one game per rating period**. The team's first loss took the rating from 1500 to 1305, and by games 14–18 the RD had fallen to about 90 with swings of **±21–29 per game** ([tournament-dashboard.json](../data/matches/tournament-dashboard.json); [Glickman, Glicko-2](http://www.glicko.net/glicko/glicko2.pdf)). An upload keeps the rating, raises its volatility and restarts the 8-game display count ([level-4.txt](../level-4.txt)). Large edges are realised fast: stopgap_v1 took the seat from **1120 to 1481 in about 13 minutes** ([DEPLOYS.md](../DEPLOYS.md)). The game rate is ≈95 per hour per seat: 100.4 games per minute × 2 seats ÷ 127 active seats. The live protocol is short. Space uploads at least 20 games apart (~13 min). Watch the first 20–30 games of each upload per colour, and roll back if the win rate is at or below 35% over 20 games while the rating is flat or falling; re-uploading the old model keeps the rating. Never let the contest end with fewer than 8 games on the current model. The tournament dashboard keeps listing sub-8-game teams at their kept rating, but how the contest leaderboard handles them is undocumented, so only the organisers can settle it. As the rating climbs, matchmaking pairs the seat with stronger opponents (the top of the snapshot was 1943–2020), and the live win rate flattens toward 50% even when the model is improving.

## Conclusion

In an evaluator-only contest with a small fixed search, the submitted model is less a "player" than a **move-ordering function plus a calibrated win-probability estimate** that has to suit one particular PUCT with FPU 0, virtual loss and tie-breaking quirks. That explains why exact hand-built tactics beat a from-scratch network here, and why the knobs that matter are move ranks and value calibration rather than temperatures. It also means the residual-on-hand design is the natural shape for the final model: the hand features supply the exact tactics, and the learned part only has to correct ordering and calibration, the two things search-improved targets teach well.

The team's bottleneck has moved from model strength to **measurement and timing**. Three models are indistinguishable against the same sparring partner, the referee's near-determinism inflates apparent sample sizes, and the live seat yields only about 260 more rated games. The next hour gains more from a discriminating gate (paired openings against hand_v2 plus regression positions from live losses) than from any extra training. After about 16:00, only a large, clearly gated edge justifies an upload; anything smaller trades expected rating for variance.
